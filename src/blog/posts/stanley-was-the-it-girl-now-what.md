When the Stanley obsession was at its peak, I wanted one badly. I never actually ended up buying the real thing, I ended up with a sparkly dupe, I really did understand the appeal though. Technically, it was just a water bottle, yet it somehow felt like an accessory. The cute colours, cup and straw design stood out and weirdly having a ‘cute’ bottle actually made me want to drink more water. 

I still think Stanleys are cute. But if I were buying a new bottle today? I'd probably choose an Owala.

That change in my own preference reveals a bigger problem for Stanley. [*Modern Retail*,](https://www.modernretail.co/operations/brands-briefing-stanley-1913s-next-era-focuses-on-storage-international-growth-and-sports/) citing consumer-purchase data from Consumer Edge, reported in June 2026 that Stanley's US direct-to-consumer spending fell around 20% in 2025 as competition in hydration intensified. Stanley didn’t suddenly make a bad bottle. The actual question is how a brand that created an online obsession keeps giving customers a reason to choose it once that obsession cools.

[Smith and Colgate (2007)](https://www.jstor.org/stable/40470272?sid=primo&seq=1), two academic researchers writing in  the *Journal of Marketing Theory and Practice*, showed me why I wanted one in the first place. Stanley offered obvious **functional value**, but its **experiential** and **symbolic value** made it different. Colours, drops and that recognisable design made hydration feel aesthetic and expressive. Stanley wasn't only competing on what its bottle *did*. It was competing on how owning one *felt*.

Looking at Stanley through the **Value Proposition** makes its next move interesting. Stanley's [February 2026 Vitalize collection](https://www.stanley1913.com/blogs/stanley-1913-newsroom/the-stanley-1913-brand-launches-new-vitalize%E2%84%A2-collection-featuring-totes-and-backpacks?) expands into totes, backpacks and other lifestyle products. But liking my Stanley-style cup doesn't make me want Stanley on my backpack. **Adding more products isn't automatically the same as adding more customer value.**

So rather than chasing another viral product, I'd use **SOSTAC** to make that value gap the starting point. A **SMART objective** could focus on increasing non-drinkware purchases among existing younger Stanley customers over six months, using Stanley's internal baseline to set an achievable numerical target.

My implementation would allow consumers digitally construct a customised Stanley "setup", by coordinating their bottle with matching colours and accessories, before showcasing it on social media. **PESO** could connect paid creator styling, earned coverage, customer sharing and Stanley's owned experience, and IMC keeps a consistent idea running through each interaction.

Most importantly, I wouldn't judge it by likes. **KPIs** such as setup-to-purchase conversion and non-drinkware purchases would show whether customers actually see value beyond the cup. Stanley could then allocate more **budget** towards the channels producing those outcomes, rather than whichever one generates the most noise.

### **Takeaway**
Stanley convinced me that a water bottle could feel like an accessory. Convincing me that ‘Stanley’ belongs on the rest of my lifestyle is a much harder sell.
